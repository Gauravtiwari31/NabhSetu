\section{Elementary aggregates: the three candidate formulae}

At the lowest level no expenditure weights exist, so the index over $N$ matched items in a
cell must be an unweighted average of prices or of price relatives. There are exactly
three standard choices.

\subsection{Carli --- arithmetic mean of price relatives}
\begin{equation}
I^{C}_{0,t} \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \frac{p_{i,t}}{p_{i,0}}
\end{equation}

\noindent \textbf{Do not use.} Carli fails the \emph{time reversal test}: in general
$I^C_{0,t}\cdot I^C_{t,0} > 1$, so it has a known upward bias that compounds when chained.
For volatile prices --- which airfares emphatically are --- the bias is large. Naming Carli
and rejecting it for this reason is a cheap way to demonstrate you know the literature.

\subsection{Dutot --- ratio of arithmetic mean prices}
\begin{equation}
I^{D}_{0,t} \;=\; \frac{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} p_{i,t}}{\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} p_{i,0}}
\;=\; \frac{\sum_i p_{i,t}}{\sum_i p_{i,0}}
\end{equation}

\noindent Dutot passes time reversal, but it \emph{implicitly weights each item by its base
price}: an expensive item dominates. Eurostat's web-scraping guidelines note this
explicitly. For airfares this is fatal: a \rt{del}--\rt{bom} quote and a
\rt{blr}--\rt{hyd} quote in the same cell would be weighted by their rupee level. Dutot is
only admissible \emph{within a homogeneous cell} where all items are genuinely comparable.

\subsection{Jevons --- geometric mean of price relatives}
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;
I^{J}_{0,t} \;=\; \prod_{i=1}^{N}\left(\frac{p_{i,t}}{p_{i,0}}\right)^{1/N}
\;=\; \exp\!\left(\frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}\ln\frac{p_{i,t}}{p_{i,0}}\right)\;}
\end{equation}

\noindent \textbf{This is the APIx elementary formula.} Justification, in the order to say
it out loud:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{MoSPI already uses it.} The CPI 2024 FAQ, Q.20: \emph{``The Jevons index
(Short index formula) is used for compiling elementary indices in the CPI 2024 series.''}
Matching the official formula is the whole integration argument.
\item \textbf{It passes time reversal and circularity/transitivity.} $I^J_{0,t}\cdot
I^J_{t,0}=1$ exactly, and $I^J_{0,1}\cdot I^J_{1,2} = I^J_{0,2}$ --- so chaining Jevons over
a fixed matched sample introduces \emph{no} chain drift.
\item \textbf{It is robust to the lognormal shape of fare distributions.} Airfares are
strongly right-skewed; taking logs approximately normalises them, and the geometric mean is
the natural centre of a lognormal.
\item \textbf{It implicitly assumes unitary elasticity of substitution}, which for a
consumer choosing between two carriers on the same sector is a far better approximation
than Dutot's zero-substitution or Carli's inconsistent assumption.
\end{enumerate}

\begin{keybox}
\bhead{Worked example --- build this as a unit test.} Cell $g$ has three matched quotes.
Base period prices $(4000, 6000, 12000)$; current period $(4400, 6000, 9000)$.
\[
I^{C} = \tfrac{1}{3}(1.10 + 1.00 + 0.75) = 0.9500
\qquad
I^{D} = \tfrac{4400+6000+9000}{4000+6000+12000} = \tfrac{19400}{22000} = 0.8818
\]
\[
I^{J} = (1.10 \times 1.00 \times 0.75)^{1/3} = (0.8250)^{1/3} = 0.9382
\]
Three formulae, the same data, a spread of 6.8 index points. \textbf{Put this exact slide
in the deck.} It is the fastest possible proof that the index formula is a decision with
consequences, not boilerplate. Note also the ordering $I^{D} < I^{J} < I^{C}$, which holds
by the AM--GM inequality whenever base prices and relatives are negatively associated.
\end{keybox}

\section{Higher-level aggregation: the weighted index family}

Above the elementary level, expenditure weights exist and the index becomes a weighted
average of elementary indices. Let $I_{g}$ be the elementary index for cell $g$ and $W_g$
its weight.

\subsection{Laspeyres --- base-period quantity weights}
\begin{equation}
I^{L}_{0,t} \;=\; \frac{\sum_g q_{g,0}\,p_{g,t}}{\sum_g q_{g,0}\,p_{g,0}}
\;=\; \sum_g s_{g,0}\,\frac{p_{g,t}}{p_{g,0}},
\qquad s_{g,0} = \frac{p_{g,0}q_{g,0}}{\sum_h p_{h,0}q_{h,0}}
\end{equation}
A Laspeyres index is a base-weighted arithmetic mean of price relatives. It overstates the
cost of living because it ignores substitution away from items that became relatively
expensive (\emph{substitution bias}).

\subsection{Paasche --- current-period quantity weights}
\begin{equation}
I^{P}_{0,t} \;=\; \frac{\sum_g q_{g,t}\,p_{g,t}}{\sum_g q_{g,t}\,p_{g,0}}
\end{equation}
Understates, for the mirror-image reason. Requires current-period quantities, which a CPI
does not have in real time.

\subsection{Fisher --- the ideal index}
\begin{equation}
I^{F}_{0,t} \;=\; \sqrt{I^{L}_{0,t}\cdot I^{P}_{0,t}}
\end{equation}
Passes essentially every index-number test (it is ``superlative''). Not operationally
available for a monthly CPI, but it is the \emph{target} that superlative approximations
aim at, and it is the natural benchmark in a back-test.

\subsection{T\"ornqvist --- the superlative workhorse}
\begin{equation}
\ln I^{T}_{0,t} \;=\; \sum_g \frac{s_{g,0}+s_{g,t}}{2}\,\ln\!\frac{p_{g,t}}{p_{g,0}}
\end{equation}
A geometric mean of price relatives with average expenditure shares as weights. This is the
index that GEKS-T\"ornqvist (below) makes multilateral, and the one the UK ONS adopted for
scanner and alternative data.

\subsection{Young / Modified Laspeyres --- what MoSPI actually uses}

A pure Laspeyres needs base-period quantities. In practice statistical offices use weights
from an earlier weight-reference period $b$ (here, HCES 2023--24) and prices from a later
price-reference period $0$ (here, 2024). Two variants:

\begin{align}
\text{\bf Young:}\quad & I^{Y}_{0,t} \;=\; \sum_g s_{g,b}\,\frac{p_{g,t}}{p_{g,0}} \\[4pt]
\text{\bf Lowe (modified Laspeyres):}\quad & I^{Lo}_{0,t} \;=\;
\frac{\sum_g q_{g,b}\,p_{g,t}}{\sum_g q_{g,b}\,p_{g,0}}
\;=\; \sum_g \frac{s_{g,b}\,\frac{p_{g,0}}{p_{g,b}}}{\sum_h s_{h,b}\frac{p_{h,0}}{p_{h,b}}}\cdot\frac{p_{g,t}}{p_{g,0}}
\end{align}

\noindent The difference is whether the base-period weights are \emph{price-updated} from
$b$ to $0$. MoSPI CPI 2024 FAQ Q.21: \emph{``The Young/Modified Laspeyres' index is used for
compiling higher level indices.''} \textbf{APIx implements the Young form as default and the
Lowe form as a configurable alternative}, because we can price-update our own route weights
using our own collected prices --- which is a small but real methodological contribution.

\begin{keybox}
\bhead{The two-line summary a judge will remember:}
\emph{``Jevons at the bottom, Young at the top --- exactly what MoSPI does. We didn't
invent a formula; we implemented theirs, and then added the dimensions they don't have.''}
\end{keybox}

\section{Chain drift, and why it matters here more than anywhere}

A \textbf{chained} index multiplies successive period-to-period links:
\begin{equation}
I^{\text{chain}}_{0,T} = \prod_{t=1}^{T} I_{t-1,t}
\end{equation}
A \textbf{direct} (fixed-base) index compares $T$ to $0$ in one step. If the formula is
transitive, they agree. If not, the difference is \textbf{chain drift}.

Drift arises when products enter and leave the sample and prices oscillate --- the textbook
generator is a promotional cycle where a price falls, sales spike, the price returns, and
the index does not. \textbf{Airfares are a pathological case}: fares bounce within a day,
routes appear and vanish, and the matched sample changes every collection. Diewert's
chapter in the IMF CPI Manual's companion volume explicitly flags airlines and hotels as
the dynamic-pricing case that motivates multilateral methods.

APIx's defence is layered:
\begin{enumerate}
\item Jevons on a \emph{strictly matched} sample within a cell is transitive, so no drift
arises from the formula itself.
\item Sample churn is handled by \textbf{re-matching on a stable product key} (\S\ref{sec:key}),
not by re-drawing the sample each day.
\item Where churn is unavoidable, we compute a \textbf{multilateral} variant and publish
the difference as a diagnostic. If the two agree, that is evidence of robustness; if they
diverge, that divergence is itself a finding worth reporting.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Multilateral index methods}

Multilateral methods compare \emph{all} periods in a window simultaneously, which makes them
transitive by construction and therefore drift-free within the window.

\subsection{GEKS (Gini--Elteto--Koves--Szulc)}

Let $P_{l,m}$ be a bilateral index between periods $l$ and $m$ computed by some formula.
Over a window of $T+1$ periods:
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;P^{\text{GEKS}}_{0,t} \;=\; \prod_{l=0}^{T}\Big(P_{0,l}\cdot P_{l,t}\Big)^{\frac{1}{T+1}}\;}
\end{equation}
That is: every period is used in turn as a \emph{bridge} between $0$ and $t$, and the
geometric mean of all bridged comparisons is taken.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{GEKS-Jevons (GEKS-J)} uses the Jevons as the bilateral input. This is the
correct choice when no expenditure weights exist --- Eurostat's HICP web-scraping guidelines
name GEKS-Jevons and the Time Product Dummy as the options for weightless web-scraped data.
\textbf{APIx uses GEKS-Jevons as its multilateral variant.}
\item \textbf{GEKS-T\"ornqvist (CCDI)} uses T\"ornqvist. ONS uses this with a 25-month
window and a mean splice for scanner and alternative data. Requires expenditure shares.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Time Product Dummy (TPD)}

A regression formulation. Pool all quotes across the window and estimate
\begin{equation}
\boxed{\;\ln p_{i,t} \;=\; \alpha \;+\; \sum_{m=1}^{T}\delta_m D^{\text{time}}_m
\;+\; \sum_{j=2}^{N}\gamma_j D^{\text{item}}_j \;+\; \varepsilon_{i,t}\;}
\end{equation}
where $D^{\text{time}}_m$ is 1 if the observation is from period $m$, and
$D^{\text{item}}_j$ is 1 if the observation is item $j$. The index is then
\begin{equation}
P^{\text{TPD}}_{0,t} \;=\; \exp(\hat\delta_t)
\end{equation}
The item dummies absorb permanent quality differences between products; the time dummies
carry the pure price movement. With a balanced panel and no weights, TPD and GEKS-Jevons
coincide. TPD's real advantage is that it \textbf{handles missing observations gracefully}
--- which for airfares (sold-out cells, cancelled routes) is exactly the situation. A
weighted variant (WTPD) uses expenditure shares as regression weights.

\begin{keybox}
\bhead{Why TPD is a strong demo.} It converts index construction into a regression, so you
can report $R^2$, residual diagnostics, and standard errors on $\hat\delta_t$ --- i.e.\
\textbf{a confidence interval on the inflation number}. Official CPI does not publish one.
Doing so is a visible, defensible piece of added value that costs about forty lines of
\code{statsmodels}.
\end{keybox}

\subsection{Geary--Khamis and QU-GK}

Geary--Khamis solves simultaneously for a price level $P_t$ and a set of quality-adjusted
unit values $v_j$:
\begin{equation}
v_j = \sum_t \frac{q_{j,t}}{\sum_m q_{j,m}}\cdot\frac{p_{j,t}}{P_t},
\qquad
P_t = \frac{\sum_j p_{j,t}q_{j,t}}{\sum_j v_j q_{j,t}}
\end{equation}
solved by iteration to a fixed point. ONS's index-method framework scored
\textbf{Quality-adjusted Geary--Khamis (QU-GK)} highest when expenditure information is
available, and recommended GEKS-Jevons in its absence. \textbf{APIx does not have
quantities, so GK is documented as future work, not implemented.} Say that plainly; a team
that names a method and says ``we chose not to implement it, here is why'' reads as more
competent than one that implements four methods badly.

\subsection{Splicing: extending a multilateral window}

A multilateral index is only transitive \emph{inside} its window. When a new period arrives
the window rolls forward, and the new window's index must be attached to the published
series without revising already-published numbers (official CPI is non-revisable). The
attachment rule is the \emph{splice}. With $P^{\text{new}}$ the freshly-estimated window of
length $T+1$:

\begin{center}
\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}L{3.0cm} L{5.6cm} X@{}}
\toprule
\bhead{Splice} & \bhead{Link factor} & \bhead{Behaviour} \\
\midrule
Movement (window) splice & $P^{\text{new}}_{T-1,T}$ & Uses only the last link. Simple, noisiest. \\
Window splice & $P^{\text{new}}_{0,T}/P^{\text{new}}_{0,T-1}$ & Links on the full window. \\
Half splice & Link on the window midpoint & Compromise; used by Statbel. \\
\textbf{Mean splice} & Geometric mean of all possible splice points & \textbf{Lowest variance; ONS's choice.} APIx default. \\
FEWS & Fixed-effects index with window splice & Regression-based analogue. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\noindent Mean splice, formally, over splice positions $j = 1,\dots,T$:
\begin{equation}
P_{0,T} \;=\; P_{0,T-1}\cdot\prod_{j=1}^{T}\left(\frac{P^{\text{new}}_{T-j,T}}{P^{\text{new}}_{T-j,T-1}}\right)^{1/T}
\end{equation}

\section{Linking a new series to an old one}

MoSPI's own back-series arithmetic is worth reproducing exactly, because APIx must be able
to express its index on the CPI 2024 base and because it is a clean, citable formula.

Let $\overline{I}^{\,\text{new}}_y$ and $\overline{I}^{\,\text{old}}_y$ be the annual
\emph{geometric mean} indices of the new and old series over a common overlap year $y$.
The linking factor is
\begin{equation}
\text{LF} \;=\; \frac{\overline{I}^{\,\text{new}}_y}{\overline{I}^{\,\text{old}}_y}
\qquad\text{and}\qquad
I^{\text{linked}}_t \;=\; I^{\text{old}}_t \times \text{LF}
\end{equation}
For CPI 2024 the overlap year was 2025 and the General-level factors were Rural 0.5222,
Urban 0.5320, Combined 0.5267.

\begin{warnbox}
\bhead{Use this in the demo.} Show APIx on its own base (APIx Jan-2026 $=$ 100), then apply
a linking factor and show it \emph{on the CPI 2024 base} next to the official air fare item
index. That single toggle communicates ``we understand how your series is actually
maintained'' better than any amount of dashboard polish.
\end{warnbox}

\section{The seasonality problem}

Airfares are violently seasonal: Diwali, Christmas--New Year, summer holidays, wedding
season, Kumbh-scale events. Three treatments, and APIx publishes all three:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Not seasonally adjusted (NSA).} The raw index. This is what CPI publishes
(``2024$=$100, NSA'').
\item \textbf{Year-on-year rate.} $\pi_t = (I_t/I_{t-12} - 1)\times 100$ --- differencing at
lag 12 removes stable seasonality without a model. This is exactly MoSPI's published
inflation definition:
\begin{equation}
\text{Inflation}(\%) \;=\; \frac{I_t - I_{t-12}}{I_{t-12}}\times 100
\end{equation}
\item \textbf{Model-based SA.} X-13ARIMA-SEATS or STL decomposition, with an explicit
\emph{moving-holiday regressor} for Diwali (which shifts across the Gregorian calendar) and
event dummies for known demand shocks. Report SA as \emph{experimental} --- a series with
under two years of history cannot support a credible seasonal factor, and saying so is the
statistically literate answer.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Sampling and variance}

\subsection{The estimator and its uncertainty}

A published index is a point estimate from a sample. Its sampling variance is estimable, and
publishing it is a differentiator. For the Jevons, work in logs where the estimator is a
simple mean:
\begin{equation}
\hat\mu_g = \frac{1}{N_g}\sum_{i} \ln\frac{p_{i,t}}{p_{i,0}},
\qquad
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\mu_g) = \frac{1}{N_g(N_g-1)}\sum_i\left(\ln\frac{p_{i,t}}{p_{i,0}} - \hat\mu_g\right)^2
\end{equation}
and by the delta method the variance on the index level $I^J_g = \exp(\hat\mu_g)$ is
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(I^J_g) \;\approx\; \big(I^J_g\big)^2\,\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(\hat\mu_g)
\end{equation}
Aggregating with weights $W_g$ (treating cells as independent strata):
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(I) \;=\; \sum_g W_g^2\,\widehat{\operatorname{Var}}(I_g)
\end{equation}

\subsection{Bootstrap intervals}

Because fare distributions are neither normal nor independent within a flight, the
analytic interval is indicative only. APIx also ships a \textbf{block bootstrap}: resample
\emph{whole flights} (not individual quotes) with replacement, $B=1000$ times, recompute
the full index pipeline, and take the $2.5$th and $97.5$th percentiles. Blocking at the
flight level preserves the within-flight correlation induced by shared inventory.

\subsection{Sample size for a target precision}

To detect a $\delta\%$ move at significance $\alpha$ with power $1-\beta$, in log space with
per-quote log-relative standard deviation $\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
N \;\geq\; \frac{2\left(z_{1-\alpha/2}+z_{1-\beta}\right)^2\sigma^2}{\left(\ln(1+\delta/100)\right)^2}
\end{equation}
Estimate $\sigma$ from the first week of live collection and \textbf{publish the resulting
minimum cell size as a design constraint} --- cells that fall below it are suppressed rather
than published noisily. Suppression rules are what official statistics does; mimicking them
signals maturity.
